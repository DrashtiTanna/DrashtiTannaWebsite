01 — Location

A beach between the airport and the Minoans

The Xenia Motel sits on Karteros Beach, a long, sandy, shallow-water coastline in the village of Karteros, a few kilometres east of Heraklion — the economic and administrative hub of Crete. Heraklion International Airport "Nikos Kazantzakis", one of the busiest in Greece, lies only ~4 km away, making the site a natural gateway between arrival and the island.

The wider region is steeped in history: the Palace of Knossos and the coastal site of Amnisos anchor the area's deep Minoan heritage, while the beach itself is a popular family destination served by tavernas, beach bars and water-sports facilities in summer.

In the 1960s Karteros was largely undeveloped agricultural land — the Xenia Motel was one of the first deliberate efforts to bring tourism to the area.

02 — Layers of history

Five millennia in one island

Crete's story runs from Europe's earliest advanced civilisation to post-war modernism — and the Xenia programme is one of its most recent layers.

c. 3000–1100 BCE

Minoan zenith

Monumental architecture and sophisticated urban planning at Knossos — whose palatial reds later echo in the Xenia's painted structure.

67 BCE – 1205

Roman & Byzantine rule

Roman conquest, Byzantine control, briefly interrupted by Arab conquest (824–961 CE).

1205–1669

Venetian period

Western European models transform Heraklion into a fortified Renaissance centre.

1669–1898

Ottoman rule

Mosques, fountains and inward-facing courtyard housing typologies shape the urban fabric.

1913

Union with Greece

Neoclassical styles mingle with vernacular traditions in pursuit of a unified Hellenic identity.

1941–1951

War & reconstruction

The Battle of Crete devastates the island; Marshall Plan aid drives post-war rebuilding and the rise of modernism.

1950

GNTO founded

The Greek National Tourism Organisation shapes a modern architectural language balancing international modernism with local materials.

1963

Xenia Karteros opens

Aris Konstantinidis completes the motel — a constellation of eight one-storey pavilions around a central courtyard.

2002

Listed monument

Recognised as a national modern monument — yet left to advanced neglect.

2025

Xenia Lunapark

This proposal: reclamation as a year-round, intergenerational public lunapark.

03 — The heritage

Konstantinidis' anonymous architecture

Completed in 1963, the Xenia Karteros is one of the most representative examples of post-war Greek architectural modernism. Eight one-storey pavilions are organised on a precise grid around a central courtyard — an introverted composition promoting interaction, rest and visual cohesion, with transitional balconies mediating between guest rooms and courtyard.

Its low profile, modular composition and local materials embed the building in the hilly coastal landscape rather than dominating it, while red-painted structural elements subtly evoke the palatial colours of nearby Minoan sites.

An "anonymous architecture" that eschewed stylistic spectacle in favour of essential human needs and environmental harmony. On Aris Konstantinidis' design ethos

Despite being listed as a national modern monument in 2002, the complex has since fallen into advanced neglect — a monument to both a visionary past and the consequences of abandonment.

04 — The vision

All year;
all ages;
all budgets.

The reconceptualisation transforms the neglected landmark into a vibrant, inclusive, intergenerational leisure destination — a trustworthy "third place" for Karteros and Heraklion.

Rather than reactivating the site for seasonal tourist gain alone, Xenia Lunapark operates throughout the year and serves families, children, teenagers, adults, seniors, locals and tourists alike — from free-to-access play areas to budget-conscious food markets, scientific initiatives and cultural events.

The project completes the unfinished mission of the original Xenia initiative: regional revitalisation — turning a relic of past development into a generative platform for contemporary communal life.

06 — Spatial strategy

From cluster to polycentric field

Konstantinidis' original scheme was hierarchical: a main pavilion cluster around one courtyard, a secondary service cluster to the south, and a corridor axis that visually connected the inland courtyard to the sea — a threshold rather than a connector.

The Lunapark dissolves this hierarchy into a polycentric layout: pavilions gain autonomy as individually programmed units, and the single courtyard finds echoes across the site in a network of social squares — each cultivating different modes of gathering, interaction and play.

A roof-terrace addition opens a new public level above the pavilions, with views over the courtyard, the beach and the Aegean horizon.

09 — Business model

A publicly owned third place

A hybrid framework of municipal oversight, local stakeholders and cultural-academic partnerships keeps the site public — revenues from commercial activities are reinvested into upkeep and programming.

Operator

Municipality of Heraklion

Day-to-day operation, maintenance, staffing and programming — protecting the site as a civic asset against privatisation.

Co-design · living lab

University of Crete

Students of Material Science & Engineering co-design the mini golf, karaoke rooms, funhouse and playground.

Food market · pastry shop

Cretan Women's Food Cooperatives

Rotating market stalls with traditional recipes and sustainable agriculture, creating local economic opportunity.

VR / AR experience

FORTH — Foundation for Research & Technology Hellas

Immersive digital content rooted in the region's science and heritage.

Escape room

Museums of Heraklion

Archaeological Museum, Natural History Museum of Crete and Kotsanas Museum of Ancient Greek Technology.

Live music · events

Cultural Association Agios Ioannis Pitsilias

Concerts, theatre, open-mic nights and community-led programming on the outdoor stage.
